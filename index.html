Research Interests

Chinese Linguistics Cantonese Dialect Historical Phonology Vernacular Chinese Fiction Folk Songs Performing Arts

I am a PhD researcher in the Department of Chinese Studies at the University of Zurich, specialising in the historical phonology of the Cantonese dialect and its rich oral literary traditions. My work sits at the intersection of historical linguistics, dialectology, and cultural heritage studies.

My doctoral research investigates the tonal and phonological systems embedded in Cantonese folk songs from the 17th to 19th centuries — a corpus that offers rare, datable evidence of the spoken language at a time of significant phonological change. I draw on classical Chinese philological methods alongside fieldwork-based approaches, combining archival analysis with the documentation of living performance traditions.

Beyond my dissertation, I am deeply interested in vernacular Chinese fiction and the performing arts as sites where dialect, literature, and community identity intersect.

Scholarly Work

Research Projects

PhD Dissertation

The Historical Origin and A Phonological Study of Cantonese Folk Songs from the 17th to 19th Centuries

This dissertation reconstructs the phonological system of Cantonese as evidenced in folk song texts — chiefly mùyúgē (wooden-fish songs) and related genres — produced between the early Qing Dynasty and the late 19th century. By combining close textual philology with systematic tonal analysis, the project traces how the Cantonese sound system evolved during this critical period and how folk song conventions both reflected and preserved archaic phonological features.

Fieldwork Project

Between Tradition and Modernity — The Legacy and Revival of Mùyúgē and Lóngzhōugē

Interviews & Audio Recording · May – July 2024

This fieldwork project documents the contemporary transmission of two endangered Cantonese folk song genres: mùyúgē (wooden-fish songs) and lóngzhōugē (dragon boat songs). Through in-depth interviews with practitioners and systematic audio recordings, the project captures how these oral traditions are being maintained, adapted, and revived in present-day communities — providing a living complement to the historical archival research of my dissertation.

Conference Paper

The Tone System of Cantonese in the Late Qing Dynasty as Reflected in Yuè'ōu

IACL-32, June 2026

This paper examines the tonal inventory of Cantonese as documented in Yuè'ōu, a late Qing Dynasty source, to reconstruct how the tonal system functioned and was perceived by educated speakers of the period. The analysis contributes to a broader diachronic account of tonal change in southern Chinese dialects.

Conference Paper

Reclassifying Cantonese Folk Songs: Conceptual Overlap in Mùyúgē, Mōyúgē, and Dàngē (1600–1900)

EACS-26, July 2026

Existing scholarship has treated mùyúgē, mōyúgē, and dàngē as largely discrete genres of Cantonese folk song. This paper challenges that classification by demonstrating significant overlap in their textual conventions, performance contexts, and phonological features across the 17th–19th centuries, arguing for a more fluid, context-dependent taxonomy.
